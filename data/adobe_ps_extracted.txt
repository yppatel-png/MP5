ABOUT ADOBE
Adobe builds software that people use to create, communicate and learn.
Photoshop, Illustrator and Premiere Pro are standard tools for design and video,
and Acrobat and PDF, a format Adobe created, are how much of the world
shares documents. Firefly, Adobe’s family of generative AI models, now works
inside many of these products, and Acrobat AI Assistant helps people read and
understand long documents. Adobe Experience Cloud helps organisations

research teams in India. This problem statement brings two of these ideas,
personalisation and AI that helps people learn, to students at their own institute.
1. PROBLEM STATEMENT
Every college student arrives with a different background and leaves with a
different ambition. A chemical engineering student may want a software role, a
first-year student may already be planning an MS abroad, and a classmate may
be preparing for the civil services or an MBA. Yet the academic journey is
largely one-size-fits-all: the same core courses, a long list of electives, minors
and open courses, and academic rules that students mostly navigate on their
own, through seniors, notice boards and word of mouth.
The challenge is to build an 
own institute: one that understands an individual student and their goal,
identifies their skill gaps, creates a personalised plan from the institute’s
curriculum and relevant open learning resources within the institute’s academic
rules, continuously adapts that plan based on progress, and helps the student
demonstrate measurable readiness for their goal.

2.2  DIAGNOSE
Understand the student’s:
•Existing knowledge and skills (assessed or self-declared)
•Academic background: programme, year, courses completed
•Strengths and weaknesses
•G oals and ambitions (career, higher studies, competitive exams)
•L earning pace and behaviour
•Constraints: available time, remaining credits and elective slots, applicable
academic rules
The system should identify gaps between the student’s current capabilities and
what their goal requires.
Profile data varies in reliability: grades and assessed results are strong
evidence, while self-declared interests and skills are weak until backed by
evidence. The system must record the source of each item and weigh it
accordingly.
2.3  PERSONALISE
Create an individualised plan by selecting:
•R elevant institute courses, electives and minors
•L earning resources (institute and open)
•Exercises and practice activities
•Assessments
•M ilestones beyond coursework, such as projects, internships and exam
timelines
Every plan must satisfy all constraints (credit limits, prerequisites, semester
offerings, eligibility rules, student preferences such as a credit cap or courses
not to be taken together, and available time) and be verifiable against them. It
must not assign content the student has already mastered. Practice should be
active (exercises, projects, case studies) rather than passive content
consumption.

2.4  ADAPT AND EV ALUATE MASTERY
The learning plan should not remain static. The student should be assessed
continuously through quizzes, exercises and project checkpoints, not only one-
off tests, and the plan should adapt based on signals such as:
•A ssessment scores
•E xercise performance
•N umber of attempts
•Time spent
•Topics repeatedly missed
•C ourse module completion
For example, a student who demonstrates mastery of a topic could skip
additional introductory material, while a student struggling with the same topic
could receive additional explanations, exercises, prerequisite content or open
learning resources.
Plans span multiple semesters, and goals can change. When stated interest and
evidence diverge (e.g. a student targeting data science with a weak grade in
linear algebra), the system must surface the gap, adjust the plan to close it, and
help the student reassess the goal, rather than ignore either signal.
Throughout, the system should determine whether the student has actually
acquired the target skill, distinguishing between:
“The student completed the course” vs. “The student demonstrated the
required level of proficiency.”
For goal-level outcomes such as placement readiness, mastery may be shown
through projects, timed mock tests or mock interviews rather than course
grades alone.
